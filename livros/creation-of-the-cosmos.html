Game Master's Book

Creation Of The Cosmos

Source: COC

Tales To Eternity - Creation Of The Cosmos

This is the Game Master's Book. The Player's Book (Fragments Of The Cosmos) teaches how to play; this book teaches how to run the game, and shows where the system's numbers come from so you can create your own content.

Everything here is a guideline, not a law. Use what helps your table and leave the rest.

Chapter 1 — Being the GM

The GM's Role

The Game Master (GM) runs the world. You describe what the characters see and hear, play every NPC and monster, decide when a Test is needed, and apply the rules. The players control their characters; you control everything else.

You are not the players' enemy. Your job is to give them challenges worth overcoming and a story worth remembering.

The Golden Rule

The most important rule is to have fun, and that includes you.

When a rule gets in the way of the fun, or no rule covers the situation, use common sense and make a ruling. Make it quickly, keep it consistent, and check the book after the session if needed.

The Group

The game is built for 4 to 5 characters. A balanced group usually has:

  • Tank: Takes the hits and protects allies.
  • Support: Heals and strengthens the group.
  • Physical Damage: Deals damage with weapons.
  • Magical Damage: Deals damage with spells.
  • Extra: More damage or utility.

A group without one of these roles still works, but it will feel the gap. A group without a Support, for example, depends more on potions and rests.

Smaller or Larger Groups

  • 3 players: Use easier encounters, or let one player control an extra character or a friendly NPC.
  • 6 or more players: Use harder encounters, and keep turns short so nobody waits too long.

Session Zero

Before the campaign begins, gather the group for a Session Zero. Use it to:

  • Present the campaign: its tone, setting, and what kind of story it will be.
  • Create the characters together, so they have reasons to know each other and roles that complement each other.
  • Agree on expectations: how much combat, social interaction, and puzzles the group wants, and how often you will play.
  • Present your house rules, if you have any.

Chapter 2 — Tiers & Pillars

The Four Tiers

The 12 levels are divided into 4 tiers. Each tier changes the scale of the story: who the characters are, and what they face.

Tier Levels Name
1 1–3 Adventurers
2 4–6 Heroes
3 7–9 Champions
4 10–12 Legends

Tier 1 — Adventurers

The characters are beginners. Their problems are local: bandits on the road, a beast near the village, a missing person. A bad fight can still kill them, and a handful of coins matters.

Tier 2 — Heroes

The characters are known in their region. They deal with threats to cities and kingdoms: cults, warlords, dangerous ruins. People begin to ask them for help by name.

Tier 3 — Champions

The characters are among the strongest in the world. They face threats to entire nations and lands: ancient creatures, armies, powers that few can stop.

Tier 4 — Legends

The characters are the world's last line of defense. They face threats of universal scale, but remain mortal; they are strong heroes, not gods. Their actions will be told for ages.

Pillars

The game rests on three pillars. A good session usually touches more than one.

Combat

Fights against monsters and enemies, resolved with Initiative and turns. Combat is the most structured pillar and the one the rules cover the most. Aim for about one combat per session, lasting around 5 rounds.

Social

Talking, convincing, lying, negotiating, and threatening. The players speak with NPCs, but the result always comes from a Test, using the NPC's Disposition (see Fragments Of The Cosmos, Chapter 7). Good roleplay can make the Test easier, never skip it.

Puzzle

Riddles, traps, mysteries, locks, and obstacles that require thinking. The players think and propose solutions, but the character still makes a Test to carry them out. A player who knows the answer can make the Test easier, never skip it. Tests can also give hints when the group is stuck.

Balancing the Pillars

Ask in Session Zero which pillars the group enjoys most, and lean toward them. Even so, keep a little of each: a group that only fights gets tired of combat, and a group that only talks loses tension.

A single situation can use all three pillars. A guarded door can be forced open (Combat), opened by convincing the guard (Social), or by finding the hidden mechanism (Puzzle). Let the players choose.

Chapter 3 — Running the Session

Preparation

You don't need to prepare everything, only what the players will probably face. Before a session, prepare:

  • A goal: What the session is about and where it can lead.
  • Scenes: Two or three important places or situations, with a short description of each.
  • NPCs: The names and goals of the NPCs who will appear (see Chapter 5).
  • Encounters: The monsters and enemies of the session, ready to use.
  • A hook: Something interesting for the end of the session, so the players want to come back.

Prepare situations, not solutions. Don't decide how the players will solve a problem; decide only what the problem is.

Improvisation

The players will always do something you didn't expect. When that happens:

  • Say yes, if it makes sense. If the idea is possible, let them try it, even if it wasn't in your plan.
  • Use what you have. Reuse an NPC, place, or encounter you prepared, changing only the name or the details.
  • Ask for a Test. When you are unsure of the outcome, let the dice decide.
  • Take a break. If you need a few minutes to think, it's fine to ask for them.

Write down everything you improvise. An NPC you invented on the spot can become an important part of the story.

Player Freedom

The players are free to choose what their characters do, where they go, and how they solve problems. Don't force them down a single path; if they leave your plan, adapt it.

Freedom has limits, though:

  • Consequences: Every action has consequences in the world. Attacking a guard in the middle of the city brings more guards.
  • Common sense: An action must be possible for the character in that situation.

Pacing

Keep the session moving. If a scene starts to drag, end it or move to the next one. If the players are having fun, let it last.

  • About one combat per session is a good rhythm.
  • Alternate between moments of tension (combat, danger, chases) and calm (conversation, exploration, rest).
  • Skip what isn't interesting. A trip without events can be resolved in one sentence.

Time

Time moves at different speeds depending on what is happening.

Scale Used for
Round (~10 seconds) Combat and other moments where every action counts
Minutes Exploring a room, a conversation, solving a puzzle
Hours Exploring a dungeon or a city, a short trip, a Rest
Days Travel, a Recover, Downtime
Weeks or more Long journeys, time between adventures

Tell the players clearly when time passes ("Three days later, you arrive at the city"). Keep track of time when it matters: rests, deadlines, nourishment, and effects with a duration.

Countdowns

A Countdown tracks a deadline or a growing danger. It has a starting number and a trigger that lowers it by 1. When it reaches 0, the event happens.

  • Number: Usually 2 to 6. Lower means more urgent.
  • Trigger: What lowers it: each round, each hour, each failed Test, each time the characters make noise.
  • Event: What happens at 0: the ritual is completed, the guards arrive, the cave collapses.

The Ritual (4): Lowers by 1 at the end of each round. At 0, the demon is summoned.

Show the Countdown to the players when their characters would know about it; it creates tension and pushes them to act. Keep it hidden when the danger is a surprise.

Countdowns pair well with Extended Tests: the characters must reach their successes before the Countdown reaches 0.

Calling for Tests

The Dice Decide

In Tales To Eternity, the dice decide. Even if the player knows the answer to a puzzle, or plays out a very convincing conversation, the character still makes a Test to see the result. The player's idea or roleplay can make the Test easier (granting Advantage or lowering the Difficulty), but it never replaces the roll.

Only simple actions, like opening an unlocked door or walking down a road, need no Test. If something is impossible, it just doesn't happen.

To choose the Difficulty, ask yourself how hard the task is for an ordinary trained person:

Difficulty DC Example
Easy 8 Climbing a knotted rope
Normal 11 Picking a simple lock
Hard 14 Calming a frightened horse in the middle of a fight
Very Hard 17 Jumping across a 6-meter gap
Nearly Impossible 20 Convincing a king to give up his throne

The Difficulties don't rise with tier. The characters truly get better; the hardest challenges simply appear more often in higher tiers.

  • Approach: The player describes what the character does, and you choose the Attribute (and Skill) that fits that approach. Different approaches can bring different consequences.
  • Secret Tests: Roll the Test yourself, hidden from the players, when knowing the result would reveal something the character doesn't know: noticing a hidden danger (Perception), reading a liar (Insight), or recalling information that may be wrong (Lore). Tell the player only what the character perceives.
  • Retrying: Decide whether a failed Test can be retried. Usually it can, if the situation allows and the approach changes.
  • Failure moves the story: A failure should not only mean "nothing happens". It can bring a cost, a complication, or a new path.

Chapter 4 — Narration

Narration is how you bring the world to life. There are three ways to do it, and a good GM mixes all three.

Describing

Describing is telling the players what their characters perceive: places, creatures, sounds, smells, and the results of their actions.

  • Be short. Two or three strong details are better than a long paragraph. "A cold, damp cellar. Something drips in the dark, and it smells of rot."
  • Use the senses. Don't describe only what is seen; sounds and smells make a scene real.
  • Describe the results. When a character hits, misses, or fails a Test, say what happens, not just the number. "Your blade cuts through its shoulder, and it howls in pain."
  • Leave room for questions. Describe the important parts and let the players ask about the rest.

Acting

Acting is speaking as an NPC, in the first person, as if you were them. You can use voices, accents, expressions, and gestures.

"Gold? You think I'll open this gate for a handful of gold?"

Acting makes NPCs memorable and makes the players feel part of the scene. Use it in important conversations and with the NPCs who matter. You don't need to be an actor: a single detail, like a way of speaking or a repeated expression, is enough.

Telling

Telling is reporting what an NPC says or does, in the third person, without acting it out.

The guard says he won't open the gate for so little gold.

Telling is quick and clear. Use it for unimportant NPCs, for long or technical information, to speed up a scene, or whenever you don't feel like acting.

Choosing How to Speak

There is no right choice; use what fits the moment.

Use When
Describing Scenes, places, creatures, and results of actions
Acting Important NPCs and tense or emotional conversations
Telling Minor NPCs, summaries, and moments that need speed

The players can do the same with their characters. Some prefer to act, others prefer to tell what their character says. Both are fine, and neither changes the result of a Test (see The Dice Decide, Chapter 3).

Chapter 5 — NPCs

Non-player characters (NPCs) are every person and creature in the world controlled by the GM: allies, villains, merchants, guards, and everyone in between.

Quick NPCs

To create an NPC quickly, define four things:

  • Name: Easy to say and to remember.
  • Goal: What the NPC wants. Every NPC wants something, even if it's small, like ending their shift in peace.
  • Trait: One detail that makes them stand out: a look, a habit, a way of speaking.
  • Secret: Something they hide or know. Not every NPC needs one, but it makes them interesting.

Bram, innkeeper. Goal: Pay off his debts. Trait: Laughs at his own jokes. Secret: He sells information about his guests to the thieves' guild.

Most NPCs only need a name and a trait. Save the goal and the secret for the ones who matter.

NPC Sheet

An NPC doesn't need a full sheet like a character. Write down only what is relevant to their role in the story:

  • Name, role, and Disposition.
  • Notable Attributes and Skills: Only the ones the NPC will probably use. Any Attribute or Skill you didn't write down is 0.
  • Combat: Only if the NPC will fight. In that case, use the rules from the Monster Book.

Bram, innkeeper. Disposition: Normal. Charisma +2, Deception +1.

A merchant needs Charisma and Persuasion, not Strength. A city guard who may fight needs combat statistics; the innkeeper doesn't.

Disposition

Every NPC has a Disposition toward the characters, which changes the Difficulty of social Tests against them (see Fragments Of The Cosmos, Chapter 7):

Disposition Difficulty The NPC...
Opposed +4 Wants to harm or get rid of the characters
Reluctant +2 Doesn't trust them and won't help without a reason
Normal 0 Has no strong opinion about them
Receptive −2 Likes them and is willing to help
Supportive −4 Trusts them and goes out of their way to help

Disposition changes with the characters' actions. Helping an NPC, paying them well, or sharing their goal can improve it by one step; insulting, threatening, or betraying them can lower it. A threat might work now, but the NPC won't forget it.

Recurring NPCs

Some NPCs appear again and again: a patron, a rival, a merchant, a villain. They make the world feel alive and give the story continuity.

  • Change them. Recurring NPCs react to what the characters did. A saved merchant gives discounts; a humiliated rival seeks revenge.
  • Keep notes. Write down each important NPC's name, goal, Disposition, and what they know.
  • Give them a life. NPCs keep pursuing their goals when the characters aren't around. When the group comes back, something may have changed.
  • Let the players choose. If the players love an NPC you improvised, bring them back.

Chapter 6 — Stories & Campaigns

A story is a single adventure, usually lasting one to a few sessions. A campaign is a series of stories played with the same characters.

Building a Story

Every story can be built in three parts:

  1. Hook: What draws the characters into the story. A job offer, a cry for help, a strange event, a personal reason. The best hooks connect to a character's goals or past.
  2. Conflict: The problem at the center of the story, and who or what causes it. Give the players obstacles across the three pillars (Chapter 2) and at least one difficult choice.
  3. Climax: The decisive moment, often the biggest fight or the hardest choice of the story. Its outcome should change something in the world.

Hook: Farmers from a village are disappearing at night. Conflict: A cult hides in the old mine and uses the farmers in a ritual; the village chief protects the cult in exchange for gold. Climax: The characters interrupt the ritual and must decide what to do with the chief.

Leave room for the players' choices. A story is a situation, not a script.

Finite and Endless Campaigns

Finite Campaign

A campaign with a planned ending: a great threat to defeat or a goal to reach. It works well for groups with limited time or that like a closed story.

  • Know the final threat or goal from the start, even if the players don't.
  • Plan the tiers the campaign will cover. Not every campaign needs to reach level 12.
  • Each story should bring the characters closer to the end.

Endless Campaign

A campaign without a planned ending, made of stories that follow one another. It works well for groups that play regularly and like to explore.

  • Let the world and the characters' goals create new stories.
  • Mix independent stories with longer threads that cross several of them.
  • When the characters reach level 12, the campaign can end, or the group can keep playing Legends.

Arcs

An arc is a group of stories connected by the same threat or goal. Arcs organize long campaigns and fit well with the tiers: each tier can be one arc, with its own threat and its own climax.

An arc's final villain can appear early, influencing smaller stories before the characters face them directly.

Connecting Everything

  • Characters: Use the characters' pasts, goals, and relationships as a source of hooks.
  • Consequences: What the characters did in one story affects the next. A spared enemy returns; a saved city becomes an ally.
  • Recurring elements: NPCs, places, and symbols that appear in different stories make the campaign feel like a single world.
  • Clues: Plant small details of future threats in earlier stories. When the threat arrives, the players will remember.
  • Notes: Keep a record of what happened in each session. It helps you connect stories and remember what the players did.

Chapter 7 — Rewards

Rewards show the characters that their efforts matter. There are three main kinds: experience, money, and items.

Experience or Milestones

There are two ways to level up the characters. Choose one in Session Zero.

Experience

The characters gain Experience (XP) from encounters and challenges. The XP needed to reach the next level is that level × 100 (see Fragments Of The Cosmos, Chapter 2). The XP given by each encounter is in the Monster Book.

You can also give XP for overcoming social challenges and puzzles, and for reaching important goals. As a reference, a Normal challenge gives the same XP as a Normal encounter.

Milestones

The characters level up when they reach an important point in the story: the end of a story, the defeat of a villain, the conclusion of a goal. As a reference, a group levels up after about four Normal encounters or equivalent challenges.

Milestones are simpler and keep the whole group at the same level. Experience rewards each challenge overcome.

Money

Money comes from payments, treasures, and loot. Use the price reference from Fragments Of The Cosmos, Chapter 5, to decide what a reward is worth.

  • Match the tier. A reward should let the characters buy something useful for their tier. Small amounts for Adventurers, larger ones for Legends.
  • Not everything is coins. Wealth can come as goods, jewelry, art, land, or favors.
  • Give a reason to spend. Money matters when there is something worth buying: better equipment, potions, information, or a base.

Items

Items are rewards the characters can use right away: potions, better weapons and armor, and useful equipment.

  • Potions are the easiest reward to give, and help a group without a Support.
  • Equipment can replace what the characters already have, or give them new options.
  • Special items with unique properties should be rare and memorable. Tie them to the story: the sword of a fallen hero, the shield of a destroyed kingdom.

Chapter 8 — Standards

Tales To Eternity standardizes its numbers so that everything works the same way. This chapter shows where the numbers come from, so you can create your own races, classes, spells, and items, or change the rules without breaking the system.

Tier Notation

Values that change with tier use the notation Tier 1 > Tier 2 > Tier 3 > Tier 4. A feature gained after Tier 1 only lists values starting from the tier in which it's gained (a Tier 2 feature: +2 > +3 > +4).

Quantities prefer even numbers or multiples of 5.

Frequencies

Frequency Recharges
At Will Always
Per Combat At the end of each combat
Per Rest On a Rest or a Recover
Per Recover Only on a Recover

Life and Mana

Life

Each class has a Life category, which represents how many hits it can take:

Category Hits
Fragile 3
Weak 4
Medium 5
Strong 6

Life = Hits × (3.75 + 0.5 × (level − 1)), rounded up.

In other words, starting Life is Hits × 3.75, and each level adds half the Hits.

Mana

  • Caster: 8 MP + 3 per level after the first.
  • Half-Caster: 4 MP + 1.5 per level after the first.

A Caster can Conjure about 8 Spells of their highest tier per Recover. A class without Spells can have its own Resource (like Combo). Rest and Recover restore only Life and Mana, so each Resource explains how it is gained and lost (Combo, for example, is built in combat and lost when it ends).

Damage and Healing

Damage has four categories: Weak, Medium, Strong, and Brutal. The category stays fixed; the tier decides the dice.

Weapon Damage

Also used by monsters and for healing.

Category Tier 1 Tier 2 Tier 3 Tier 4
Weak d4 (2.5) d6 (3.5) d8 (4.5) d10 (5.5)
Medium d6 (3.5) d8 (4.5) d10 (5.5) d12 (6.5)
Strong d8 (4.5) d10 (5.5) d12 (6.5) 3d4 (7.5)
Brutal 2d4 (5) 2d6 (7) 2d8 (9) 2d10 (11)
  • Light weapon: Weak.
  • One-handed weapon: Medium.
  • Larger one-handed weapon, or Versatile in two hands: Strong.
  • Heavy two-handed weapon: Brutal.

Extreme damage is the same category of the next tier. Anything above Brutal (2d12, 3d8...) is epic, only for very special things.

Use different dice with the same average only when they change the feel (d12 instead of 2d6). Never mix dice (d4 + d8).

Spell Damage

Spells deal about +1 per die compared to weapons (+2 for Brutal). This table is also used for Mana recovery.

Category Tier 1 Tier 2 Tier 3 Tier 4
Weak d6 (3.5) d8 (4.5) d10 (5.5) d12 (6.5)
Medium d8 (4.5) d10 (5.5) d12 (6.5) 3d4 (7.5)
Strong d10 (5.5) d12 (6.5) 3d4 (7.5) 2d8 (9)
Brutal 2d6 (7) 2d8 (9) 2d10 (11) 2d12 (13)

Healing

Healing uses the weapon table. A heal of one category undoes a hit of the same category.

Healing Category
Touch, one target Strong
Ranged, one target Medium
Several targets or an area Weak for each
Reaction or continuous Weak

Validation

No one goes down from a single ordinary weapon hit of their own tier: Brutal's maximum is always lower than a Fragile character's Life. A Fragile character takes about 3 Strong hits to go down, and a Strong character about 6. Brutal spell damage only appears with risk (see Spell Categories).

Defense and Hit Chance

Defense comes from armor and rises with tier, without adding Agility.

Armor Tier 1 Tier 2 Tier 3 Tier 4
None 10 11 12 13
Light 12 13 14 15
Medium 13 14 15 16
Heavy 14 15 16 17
  • Fixed attack bonuses from features: at most +2 in total.
  • Each point of Attribute or Defense is worth 5% of hit chance. Advantage adds about 15–18%.
  • Goals: A character hits a monster of their tier 65–70% of the time; a monster hits a character in Medium armor about 60% of the time.
  • The Mage is easy to hit on purpose; their protection comes from spells and features.

Spell Categories

To choose a spell's damage category, start from its type:

Type Base Category
Attack, single target Strong
Test, single target Medium
Small area (up to 2 Zones) Medium
Large area (3+ Zones) Weak
  • −1 category if it also causes a Condition or pushes.
  • +1 category if it has real risk (touch range, extra cost, affects the caster).
  • Brutal only appears with risk: a single-target attack (Strong) that also has a risk. The touch spells of Fragments Of The Cosmos, like Scorching Touch and Withering Touch, are Brutal because the caster must get close to the enemy. Features can also raise a spell to Brutal at a cost, like the Mage's Overcharge.

Area spells use a Test against 10 + Conjuring Attribute. By default, a creature that succeeds takes half damage and doesn't suffer the Condition.

Templates

Race

  • Text: Origin, Description, Size, Height, Weight, Lifespan, Speed, and 2 Languages.
  • Features: 2 small features that fit the race. They never give Attributes and never scale with tier.
  • Features can be situational (Advantage on one kind of Test, a terrain, a sense) or small combat numbers (once per Rest effects).

Class

  • Defines: Life category, Mana (Caster or Half-Caster) or its own Resource, Conjuring Attribute, number of Skills (usually 3), and starting equipment.
  • Class features: Exactly one at levels 1, 4, 7, and 10.
  • Specialization: Chosen at level 2, with features at levels 2, 5, 8, and 11. Each specialization has an implied role (tank, support, or damage).
  • Spells: Casters learn 4 Spells at levels 1, 4, 7, and 10; Half-Casters learn 2.
  • Attacks: At most 2 attacks per turn, and only for some classes. Classes without a second attack grow in other ways.

House Rules

House rules are rules your group creates or changes. They're welcome, as long as everyone agrees and knows them before they come into play (see Session Zero, Chapter 1).

Before creating one, check it against the standards in this chapter. If a new feature deals more damage than its category, or a bonus breaks the +2 attack limit, the rule will probably unbalance the game. Test it for a few sessions, and change it if it isn't working.
